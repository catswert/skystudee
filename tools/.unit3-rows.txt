Developmental Psychology|Scientific study of physical, cognitive, emotional, and social changes across the lifespan
Stability and Change|Examining which characteristics persist and which change across life
Nature and Nurture|Interaction of genetic inheritance and life experiences in shaping development
Continuous Development|Development occurring gradually through steady, cumulative change
Discontinuous Development|Development progressing through distinct stages with qualitative changes
Cross-Sectional Research|Comparing different age groups at one point in time
Longitudinal Research|Following the same individuals over time to study developmental change
Teratogens|Prenatal exposures, such as alcohol or infections, that can harm fetal development
Developmental Milestones|Skills or behaviors typically achieved within certain age ranges
Prenatal Development|Growth and development from conception until birth
Fine Motor Coordination|Control of small muscles for precise actions like writing or buttoning
Gross Motor Coordination|Control of large muscles for movement like walking, jumping, and running
Maturation|Biologically guided growth producing orderly developmental changes
Reflexes|Automatic, inborn responses supporting newborn survival
Rooting Reflex|Infant turns toward a cheek touch to find food
Visual Cliff|Glass-covered apparent drop used to test infants’ depth perception
Critical Periods|Narrow developmental windows especially important for acquiring particular abilities
Sensitive Periods|Times when learning particular skills is easier, although possible later
Imprinting|Rapid early attachment to the first moving stimulus, often seen in birds
Growth Spurt|Rapid increase in height and weight during adolescent development
Puberty|Hormonal and physical changes leading to sexual maturity
Primary Sex Characteristics|Reproductive organs and structures directly involved in reproduction
Secondary Sex Characteristics|Puberty-related physical traits not directly involved in reproduction
Menarche|First menstrual period during puberty
Spermarche|First production of sperm during puberty
Menopause|Natural end of menstrual cycles and reproductive fertility, often around age 50
Sex|Biologically influenced characteristics associated with male and female bodies
Gender|Culturally associated attitudes, feelings, behaviors, and expectations related to sex
Socialization|Learning cultural norms, behaviors, and roles through social interaction
Jean Piaget|Psychologist who proposed four stages of children's cognitive development
Schemas|Mental frameworks organizing information based on existing knowledge and experiences
Assimilation|Fitting new experiences into existing schemas
Accommodation|Changing schemas to incorporate new information
Sensorimotor Stage|Piaget’s birth-to-two stage of learning through senses and actions
Object Permanence|Understanding objects exist even when out of sight
Preoperational Stage|Piaget’s ages-two-to-seven stage of symbolic thought without consistent logical reasoning
Mental Symbols|Internal representations standing for objects, people, and events
Pretend Play|Imaginative role-playing or acting out situations, reflecting symbolic thinking
Conservation|Understanding quantity remains constant despite changes in appearance or shape
Reversibility|Mentally reversing an action or operation to its original state
Animism|Attributing life or feelings to nonliving objects
Egocentrism|Difficulty understanding perspectives different from one's own
Theory of Mind|Understanding other people have their own thoughts, feelings, and beliefs
Concrete Operational Stage|Piaget’s ages-seven-to-eleven stage of logical thinking about tangible situations
Formal Operational Stage|Piaget’s roughly age-twelve-plus stage of abstract and hypothetical reasoning
Lev Vygotsky|Psychologist emphasizing social interaction, language, and culture in cognitive development
Scaffolding|Temporary guided support gradually removed as learners gain independence
Zone of Proximal Development|Difference between what a learner can do alone and with guidance
Crystallized Intelligence|Accumulated knowledge and skills gained through education and experience
Fluid Intelligence|Reasoning and solving unfamiliar problems without relying on learned knowledge
Dementia|Cognitive decline interfering with daily functioning, including memory and judgment
Language|System of communicating meaning through symbols, sounds, and rules
Phonemes|Smallest contrasting speech sounds that distinguish word meanings
Morphemes|Smallest language units that carry meaning
Semantics|Meaning of words, phrases, and sentences in context
Grammar|System of language rules governing meaningful word combinations
Syntax|Rules governing word order and sentence structure
Nonverbal Gestures|Hand or body movements communicating without speech, such as pointing or waving
Cooing|Early infant vowel-like sounds such as “oo” and “ah”
Babbling|Repeated consonant-vowel sounds such as “ba-ba,” usually beginning around six months
One-Word Stage|Around age one, single words communicate whole ideas
Telegraphic Speech|Brief, meaningful phrases omitting less essential words
Overgeneralization of Language Rules|Applying grammatical patterns too widely, such as saying “goed”
Ecological Systems Theory|Bronfenbrenner’s model of interacting environmental layers affecting child development
Microsystem|Immediate settings and relationships, including family, peers, and school
Mesosystem|Connections among a child’s immediate settings, such as family-school relationships
Exosystem|Indirect influences, such as parental workplaces and community decisions
Macrosystem|Broader cultural values, societal norms, and economic conditions
Chronosystem|Life transitions and historical changes affecting development over time
Authoritarian Parenting|High demands and strict rules with little warmth or discussion
Authoritative Parenting|Clear expectations combined with warmth, responsiveness, and encouraged independence
Permissive Parenting|Warm and responsive approach with few firm limits or demands
Attachment Styles|Patterns of emotional bonding between children and caregivers
Secure Attachment|Comfortable exploration and trust that caregivers will provide support
Insecure Attachment|Attachment patterns marked by uncertainty, avoidance, or inconsistent responses to caregivers
Avoidant Attachment|Little outward separation distress and avoidance of caregiver on reunion
Anxious Attachment|Intense separation distress and difficulty being comforted on reunion
Disorganized Attachment|Contradictory, confused, or fearful responses toward a caregiver
Temperament|Early-emerging traits governing activity, emotional reactivity, and adaptability
Separation Anxiety|Distress when an infant or child is separated from a caregiver
Contact Comfort|Security and comfort gained from warm, physical contact with caregivers
Parallel Play|Children play alongside others without direct coordinated interaction
Imaginary Audience|Adolescent belief that others constantly watch and judge them
Personal Fable|Adolescent belief that their experiences are unique and they are invulnerable
Social Clock|Cultural expectations about the timing of major life milestones
Emerging Adulthood|Late teens through twenties, often marked by exploration of roles and goals
Erikson’s Psychosocial Stage Theory|Eight lifespan stages, each involving a social-emotional conflict
Trust vs. Mistrust|Birth–18 months: consistent care fosters security and trust
Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt|18 months–3 years: encouraged independence builds personal control
Initiative vs. Guilt|Ages 3–6: supported independent actions build initiative
Industry vs. Inferiority|Ages 6–12: developing skills and competence builds confidence
Identity vs. Role Confusion|Adolescence: exploring values and roles develops a coherent identity
Intimacy vs. Isolation|Young adulthood: building close commitments counters loneliness
Generativity vs. Stagnation|Middle adulthood: contributing to future generations supports purpose
Integrity vs. Despair|Late adulthood: accepting one’s life brings satisfaction rather than regret
Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)|Potentially traumatic childhood events including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction
Identity Achievement|Explored alternatives and made commitments to identity, values, and goals
Identity Diffusion|Neither exploring identity options nor committing to them
Identity Foreclosure|Committing to an identity without exploring alternatives
Identity Moratorium|Exploring identity options without making lasting commitments
Racial/Ethnic Identity|Sense of heritage, cultural belonging, and community membership
Sexual Orientation|Pattern of emotional, romantic, or sexual attraction to other people
Religious Identity|Sense of self shaped by spiritual beliefs, values, and faith community
Occupational Identity|Sense of self shaped by career interests, talents, and goals
Familial Identity|Sense of oneself through family relationships, roles, and expectations
Possible Selves|Imagined future versions of oneself, including hoped-for or feared identities
Gender Identity|One’s internal sense of gender, which may differ from assigned sex
Gender Roles|Social expectations for behavior and activities associated with gender
Gender Typing|Learning and adopting culturally expected gender-related behaviors and interests
Adolescent Egocentrism|Heightened self-focus and difficulty separating personal concerns from others’ attention
Social Identity|Sense of self based on membership in social groups
Two-Word Stage|Around age two, combining words into simple meaningful phrases
Kohlberg’s Moral Development Theory|Three levels of moral reasoning: preconventional, conventional, and postconventional
Preconventional Morality|Judging actions mainly by punishment avoidance or reward seeking
Conventional Morality|Judging actions by social approval, rules, and maintaining order
Postconventional Morality|Judging actions using abstract rights and ethical principles
Ainsworth’s Strange Situation|Observed infant separation and reunion behaviors to assess attachment patterns
